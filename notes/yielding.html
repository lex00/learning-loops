Yielding

A marble does one tick of work, then steps off its lane on purpose, with nothing to wait for, and goes to the back of the ramp. Later it comes back to finish. The same track as Truly parallel, the same six runtimes, and the same claw, which never comes down.

The lesson

Misconception it corrects. "Yielding is the same as waiting, and it is free." A marble that yields has nothing to wait for. It is runnable the moment it steps off, and it pays for the trip: it will not move again until the lift gets back to it. In the trace it shows as waiting for a lane, never as waiting on I/O.

What should be burned in after staring. Under asyncio and tokio the yield is the only reason the other marbles ever run. Take it away and you are back in Truly parallel, where one-dot holds its lane for three ticks. Under threads, free-threaded Python, multiprocessing, and Go the claw would have taken the marble a tick later anyway, so the polite marble just goes early, and the claw never comes down.

The six variants

Every row is a real property of the runtime, not a metaphor. The yield is the same act everywhere: the running marble leaves its lane with nothing to wait for. What differs is the word for it, and whether anything else would have made the marble leave.

Six runtimes, three schedules. The padlock schedule and the one-lane schedule are the same length as in Truly parallel, but every marble gets its first turn by tick three instead of queueing behind a marble that runs to the end. Two lanes take six ticks with or without a claw, because no marble ever runs long enough for the claw to fire. The claw hangs over the platform for the whole cycle and never comes down. Tokio is the one runtime whose cycle gets shorter: the yield puts the third task on a worker at tick two, so the straggler from Truly parallel is gone.

The skeleton and the beats

Each marble has the same authored skeleton: run one tick, yield, run two ticks. The rules are Truly parallel's, unchanged: lanes, marbles that may move at once, and a claw with a quantum of two ticks. The yield is the only new thing. When a marble's first segment ends, it steps off its lane and joins the back of the ramp, runnable, with its second segment still to do. The tables were derived by the same twenty-line simulation.

Three things fall out of the derivation. The claw never fires in any variant, because a stint of one tick and a stint of two never reach the quantum with someone waiting. Asyncio and threads keep their ten-tick cycles, so yielding did not make either faster, but every marble has moved by tick three, which is what a yield is for. And tokio drops from seven ticks to six: in Truly parallel the third task waited until both workers were free, and here the yields hand it a worker at tick two.

Morph rules

The same as Truly parallel. Toggling a variant changes the rules and the scene continues from its current state. The yield point does not move, and a marble already on the ramp keeps its mark.

  • Threads to free-threaded. The padlock vanishes and the marble at the gate moves. The claw stays up either way.
  • Any two-lane variant to asyncio. Lane two goes dashed and its marble rolls back to the front of the ramp, keeping its mark.
  • Claw appears or vanishes. Nothing visible changes in this scene, and that is the point. The legend says so.

Timing and rhythm

The yield

The marble reaches the yield point at the end of its first tick and steps straight off during the movement window: down to the ramp, then along it to the back. No claw, no cable. It leaves under its own power.

The cycle

Fourteen seconds under threads and asyncio, eight and a half under the rest. The tokio cycle is a tick shorter than in Truly parallel, and the schedule panel should make that visible next to the padlock schedule that did not change.

Deliberately left out

  • No waiting. A yield has nothing to wait for. The moment it did, it would be a trapdoor and we would be in Waiting on I/O.
  • No yield that is refused. In every runtime here, a yield goes to the back of the queue and the runtime picks the next marble. Whether the yielding marble might be picked again at once, when nothing else is runnable, is true but not drawn: the ramp is never empty when a marble yields.
  • No cooperative budget. Tokio makes its own resources yield after a budget of operations, which is a yield the task did not write. It belongs with the claw, as a runtime doing the polite thing for you, and it is not drawn.
  • No cost of the yield itself. Real yields cost a context switch, a trip through a queue, and cache. The scene charges only the wait for a lane.

Calls made

  • The yield goes to the back of the ramp, like a preempted marble, and the marble comes back to its mark. The two events look alike on purpose: a yield is a preemption the marble chose.
  • The claw is drawn and never fires. Its legend text says why. Removing it would hide the contrast the scene exists to show.
  • The skeleton yields after one tick, before the two-tick quantum, so the yield is always earlier than the claw would have been. A yield at the quantum would make the two coincide and teach nothing.
  • Schedules whose beat tables come out identical are shown once in the panel, with both labels, so "two lanes, no claw" and "two lanes and a claw" read as one row here. The manifest keeps them separate so the rules stay honest.
