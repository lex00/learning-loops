Scene design · 2026-09-06 · concurrency, scene four

Handing off

One marble makes values and one uses them, and between them sits a box with a size. A send into a full box waits. A receive from an empty box waits. The marble that waits leaves its lane holding whatever it holds, and comes back through the ramp when the other end moves. Two marbles, six runtimes, five schedules, and two new parts on the track.

The lesson

Misconception it corrects. "A queue makes them run at the same time, and sending is free." The box has a size, and both ends stall on it. A producer that gets ahead stops at a full box. A consumer that gets ahead stops at an empty one. The marble that stops gives up its lane and pays for the trip back through the ramp, exactly as a preempted marble does.

What should be burned in after staring. Under one lane the producer and consumer never move together, however big the box is: fifteen ticks with room for one value, fifteen with room for three. The work is the same fourteen ticks either way and only one marble moves at a time, so the extra room buys nothing. Two lanes bring the cycle down to eleven, which is what the consumer costs on its own. The box does not add speed. It decides who waits.

The six variants

Every row is a real property of the runtime, not a metaphor. The box is the same part everywhere: it has a size, it holds values in order, and it stops whichever end runs out of room or runs out of values. What differs is the name of the box, the words at each end, and whether the value crosses as itself or as a copy.

Six runtimes, five schedules, and a sixth schedule with no runtime on it. Threads and asyncio both take fifteen ticks, one with a padlock across two lanes and one with a single lane, which is the tie from Truly parallel showing up again. Free-threaded Python, multiprocessing, and tokio share an eleven-tick trace: two of them have a claw and one does not, and it makes no difference, because no marble here ever runs its quantum with someone waiting unless the padlock is on. Go takes eleven ticks with no box at all, and pays for it with a producer that spends four of them in a pocket. The sixth schedule is one lane with a box of three. No runtime uses it. It is in the manifest so the schedule panel can show it beside the box of one, at the same fifteen ticks.

The skeleton and the beats

The two marbles have different skeletons for the first time. The producer runs a tick, sends, and does that three times, then runs one last tick: four ticks of work. The consumer runs a tick, then receives a value and uses it for three ticks, three times: ten ticks of work. The single tick at each end is there so that no exchange ever lands on the beat where the lift raises a marble or the tray takes one.

The rules are Truly parallel's, with the box added. At a tick boundary the box is worked to a fixpoint in a fixed order: marbles already blocked in pockets before marbles that have just arrived, and receives before sends. A receive that makes room lets a blocked send in on the same beat, and with no box at all a send and a receive that meet at the same boundary pass the value hand to hand. So a marble stalls only when nothing at that boundary could serve it. A marble that does stall leaves its lane holding whatever it holds, drops into a pocket, and when the other end acts it comes back down the chute to the ramp and waits for the lift like anyone else.

In the tables the box column shows what the box holds at the end of the tick, and the pocket column shows which marble is stuck and why: full for a send that found no room, empty for a receive that found nothing, no receiver under Go, which has no box to find anything in. The first tick of every cycle is the lift raising the first marbles, so no work happens in it. The five tables were derived by the simulation, not written by hand.

Five things fall out of the derivation. One lane takes fifteen ticks whatever the box holds: one tick for the lift, then fourteen ticks of work done one at a time, four of producing and ten of consuming. Two lanes take eleven: one tick for the lift and then the consumer's ten, which is the slower side, with the producer's four fitted into the gaps. A box of one stalls somebody once when two marbles move at once, at tick four; when only one moves there are two stalls, the producer at tick three and the consumer at tick ten under asyncio, or the producer twice, at ticks three and six, under the padlock. Go's missing box stalls the producer at every send no receiver is already waiting for, which is two sends out of three and four ticks in a pocket. And the padlock's claw hands the GIL to the producer at ticks five and nine and the cycle stays at fifteen, which is what asyncio takes on one lane with no claw at all: the switch is visible in the trace and buys nothing.

Morph rules

The same as Yielding. Toggling a variant changes the rules and the scene continues from its current state. Values keep their places, and a marble in a pocket stays in it until the box lets it go.

  • Threads to free-threaded. The padlock vanishes and the marble at the gate moves. The claw stays up either way.
  • Any two-lane variant to asyncio. Lane two goes dashed and its marble rolls back to the front of the ramp, keeping its mark.
  • Claw appears or vanishes. It changes nothing except under the padlock, where it is the only reason the GIL ever moves while the ramp is empty.
  • The box shrinks. It keeps what it already holds until that is taken. A box of three holding three values, toggled to a box of one, is over its size and simply drains, and no send is served until it has room again. Toggling to Go is the same rule with a size of none.
  • The box grows. A sender blocked in a pocket is let in at once, on the toggle, without waiting for a tick boundary.
  • To multiprocessing. Shells appear on both marbles. A value already in the box stays drawn as it is, because it crossed before the rules changed; only sends made after the toggle split off a copy dot.

Timing and rhythm

The stall

The marble reaches the box at the end of its tick. If the box can serve it, the value dot flies during the movement window and the marble stays on its lane. If not, the marble drops into a pocket in the same window, holding what it holds. The pockets have no timers here. Nothing counts down, and only the other end can let a marble out.

The cycle

Fifteen ticks is twenty-one seconds at the normal speed, eleven is fifteen and a half. The two lengths are the lesson, so the schedule panel should show the fifteen of asyncio and the fifteen of threads side by side, and the box of three next to them at fifteen as well.

Deliberately left out

  • No closing of the channel and no sentinel value. Real producers say when they are finished, and the consumer's loop ends on that. Here the tray tipping is the end.
  • No cost for the copy itself. Pickling a value and pushing it through a pipe is real work and real latency under multiprocessing. The scene draws the shell and the hollow dot and charges nothing for them.
  • No second producer and no second consumer. Fan-in and fan-out change who wakes when the box moves, and that is a different scene.
  • No unbounded queue. A box with no size never stops a producer, which is the bug worth its own scene: memory grows until something falls over.
  • No select and no timeouts. Waiting on one of several boxes belongs with waiting on many at once.
  • No cancellation. A marble parked at a box that nobody will ever serve is a deadlock, and deadlocks belong after locks.

Calls made

  • Two marbles, not three. The scene is about one crossing between two ends, and a third marble would make the interesting question who gets the value rather than what the box does. It also means the tray tips after two, and the trace strip has two rows instead of three.
  • One tick of its own work at each end: the producer's last, the consumer's first. Without them an exchange would land on the same beat as the lift raising a marble or the tray taking one, and two events on one beat are two events nobody can read.
  • Receives before sends at a boundary, and marbles already in pockets before marbles that just arrived. Both orders exist so that a marble stalls only when nothing at that boundary could have served it. The visible result is a value that goes into the box and comes out again in the same tick, twice per cycle.
  • A marble standing at the gate counts as waiting for the claw. Truly parallel never needed the rule because three marbles kept the ramp full. Here there are two marbles and the ramp is empty for the whole padlock cycle, so without it the claw would never come down and the GIL would never move except when the box moved it.
  • The bigger box is a schedule with no runtime on it. It is the control: same skeletons, same one lane, three cells instead of one, and the same fifteen ticks. Naming it after a runtime would suggest some runtime is at fault for it.
  • The value is a dot that rides beside the marble rather than a mark on the marble. It has to be visibly separable, because the whole scene is about it leaving one marble and arriving at another.
  • The shell and the copy dot are drawn only for multiprocessing. Every other runtime here hands the same object across, and drawing a copy anywhere else would teach a thing that is not true.
